0. Target

(O6) Every cyclically 6-edge-connected cubic graph with oddness at most 6 has a nowhere-zero 5-flow.

Known: oddness \(\le 2\) (Jaeger 1988), oddness \(\le 4\) with cyclic 6-edge-connectivity (Mazzuoccolo–Steffen 2017, “MS”), and cyclic connectivity \(\ge \tfrac52\omega-3\) (Steffen 2010). Since a smallest counterexample to the 5-flow conjecture is cyclically 6-edge-connected (Kochol), (O6) would push its oddness to \(\ge 8\). It is a natural next step but not obviously within reach of the MS method, as explained in §2. Status (updated 2026-09-13; see §12): the fixed-2-factor template is refuted by an explicit cyclically 6-edge-connected instance on 60 vertices, so any proof of (O6) by this method must use the 0-edge or 2-factor freedom. Earlier status: the method’s obstructions are fully catalogued (§2, §6); the four-class cover problem is solved for covers by 6-cuts (Lemma 6.5, conditional on the MS uncrossing claims); the 7- and 11-cut cases are open; no counterexample to the fixed-2-factor template was found in about 60,000 instances (§4).

1. The method (Steffen 2010, MS 2017), restated

Balanced valuations (Bondy, Jaeger). \(w\colon V\to\mathbb R\) is balanced if \(|\sum_{v\in X}w(v)|\le|\partial X|\) for all \(X\). Jaeger: \(G\) has a nowhere-zero \(k\)-flow iff it has a balanced valuation of the form \(w(v)=\tfrac{k}{k-2}(2d^+(v)-d(v))\) for some orientation. For cubic \(G\) and \(k=5\): \(G\) has a nowhere-zero 5-flow iff some 2-colouring \(V=A\cup B\) (white/black) makes \(w=\mp\tfrac53\) balanced, i.e. \(5\,|b_X-a_X|\le 3\,|\partial X|\) for all \(X\), where \(a_X=|X\cap A|\), \(b_X=|X\cap B|\). Note this forces \(|A|=|B|\) and, for \(|\partial X|\le 5\), \(|b_X-a_X|\le 3\).

Flow partitions. Fix a 2-factor \(F_2\) with odd circuits \(C_1,\dots,C_{2t}\) and even circuits, and the complementary perfect matching \(M\). A canonical colouring \(c\) gives colour 1 to \(M\), colours 2,3 alternately along each circuit, except that on each odd circuit one chosen edge (the 0-edge) gets colour 0. Exactly one vertex per odd circuit misses colour 2; call them \(z_1,\dots,z_{2t}\). The subgraph \(H=c^{-1}(1)\cup c^{-1}(2)\) has all degrees \(\le2\), degree 1 exactly at the \(z_i\); so \(H\) is a disjoint union of even circuits and \(t\) paths \(P_1,\dots,P_t\) of odd length pairing up the \(z_i\) (the pairing is dictated by \(G\), \(F_2\), \(c\)).

MS build a nowhere-zero 4-flow on \(G\) plus two parallel edges per path and read off a balanced \(\pm2\) valuation. Unwinding their construction (I checked this from the definitions): the resulting black/white partition is exactly a proper 2-colouring of \(H\), each component of \(H\) coloured independently (their Lemma 2.4 is the statement that recolouring one path is allowed; recolouring an even circuit is equally allowed since the circuit orientations in the 4-flow are arbitrary). The vertex classes of the 2-colouring of \(H\) are automatically equal in size on each component (even circuits, and odd-length paths have an even number of vertices), so \(|A|=|B|\).

So the method is: choose \(F_2\), choose the 0-edges, choose a 2-colouring of each component of \(H\), set \(w=\pm\tfrac53\), and hope \(w\) is balanced. The whole freedom is \[\underbrace{\text{2-factors with }2t\text{ odd circuits}}_{\text{huge}}\times\underbrace{\prod_i|C_i|}_{\text{0-edges}}\times\underbrace{2^{\#\text{components of }H}}_{\text{colourings}} .\] MS use only the \(2^t\) path recolourings for a fixed \(F_2\) and \(c\) (and only \(t=2\)).

What can go wrong. Suppose \(w\) is not balanced, witnessed by \(S\) with \(5k>3m\), \(k=|b_S-a_S|\), \(m=|\partial S|\). Let \(c_i=|\partial S\cap c^{-1}(i)|\). Since colour-1 and colour-2 edges are bichromatic, \(k\le c_1\) and \(k\le c_2+q\), where \(q\) is the colour imbalance among the \(z\)’s inside \(S\); also \(k\equiv m\pmod 2\). Adding, \(2k\le m+q\), hence \(q>m/5\), and since \(q\le t\) we get \(m<5t\): bad cuts have fewer than \(5t\) edges. Steffen’s Proposition 1 refines this by residues mod 5, and with cyclic 6-edge-connectivity MS show for \(t=2\) that a bad cut has exactly 6 edges, 4 of colour 1 and 2 of colour 2, and separates the \(z\)’s into two same-coloured pairs.

2. What changes at oddness 6 (\(t=3\))

  • Bad cut sizes. \(m<15\); Steffen’s Proposition 1 with \(\omega=6\) gives \(m\le11\) if \(m\equiv1\), \(m\le7\) if \(m\equiv2\), \(m\le8\) if \(m\equiv3\) (mod 5). One can do better by direct case analysis. Use: \(k>\tfrac35m\), \(k\le c_1\), \(k\le c_2+q\), \(c_1+c_2\le m\), \(q\le3\), and the two parity facts \(k\equiv m\equiv c_1\pmod 2\) (colour 1 is a perfect matching, so \(c_1\equiv|S|\equiv m\)). Running through \(m=6,\dots,14\) leaves exactly five possibilities:

    6 4 4 2 3 0 \(k=c_1\)

    11 7 7 4 3 0 \(k=c_1=c_2+q\)

    Catalogue (t = 3, cyclically 6-edge-connected): a bad cut has \((m,c_1,c_2,q)\in\{(6,4,2,2),(6,4,2,3),(6,4,1,3),(7,5,2,3),(11,7,4,3)\}\); in particular there is no bad 8-cut, and \(k=c_1\) always, which means every colour-1 edge of the cut has its \(S\)-end black; where \(k=c_2+q\) is tight, also every colour-2 cut edge has its \(S\)-end black and every \(z\) in \(S\) is black. The experiments of §4 found every one of these five types and nothing else.

  • The colour imbalance \(q\) of \(S\cap Z\): paths of \(H\) with both ends in \(S\) contribute 0; a path \(P_i\) with exactly one end in \(S\) contributes \(\varepsilon_i\sigma_i=\pm1\) where \(\varepsilon_i\) is the chosen colouring of \(P_i\) and \(\sigma_i\) records which end is in \(S\). So \(q\ge2\) needs at least two paths separated by \(\partial S\) with equal signs; \(q=3\) needs all three separated with equal signs.

  • Constraint system. Write \(J(S)\subseteq\{1,2,3\}\) for the paths separated by \(\partial S\). A cut with \(J=\{a,b\}\) is bad for the colourings with \(\varepsilon_a\varepsilon_b=\sigma_a\sigma_b=:s(S)\); a cut with \(J=\{1,2,3\}\) is bad for the colourings with \(\varepsilon_1\sigma_1=\varepsilon_2\sigma_2=\varepsilon_3\sigma_3\). Up to global complement there are 4 colourings \(\varepsilon\) of the three paths. The method (with \(F_2,c\) fixed and only path recolourings) succeeds iff the system of “forbidden” relations has a solution. It has no solution in exactly these situations:

    1. two bad cuts with the same pair \(J=\{a,b\}\) and opposite signs \(s\) (this is the only obstruction at \(t=2\), and MS’s whole proof is that it cannot happen);
    2. three bad cuts with \(J=\{1,2\},\{1,3\},\{2,3\}\) and \(s_{12}s_{13}s_{23}=+1\) (each forbids one parity; the three forced parities are inconsistent);
    3. combinations with \(J=\{1,2,3\}\) cuts (each forbids two of the eight \(\varepsilon\), i.e. one class up to complement) together with pair cuts.

  • Why the MS argument does not transfer. Their final contradiction takes two bad cuts \(E_3,E_4\) for \(J=\{1,2\}\) with opposite signs, uncrosses them into \(U_1,\dots,U_4\) using cyclic 6-edge-connectivity to force \(|\partial(U_i,U_j)|\in\{0,3\}\), and then observes that \(E_3'\cup E_4'\) is a 6-cut inside \(H\) crossed an odd number of times by \(P_1\cup P_2\) while every circuit of \(H\) crosses it evenly. With a third path \(P_3\) in \(H\), \(P_3\) may cross \(E_3'\cup E_4'\) an odd number of times, and the parity contradiction disappears. So even obstruction 1 needs a new argument at \(t=3\), and obstructions 2 and 3 are new.

  • Extra freedom not used by MS. The 0-edge on each odd circuit (which moves the \(z_i\) around the circuit and changes the paths), the colourings of the even circuits of \(H\), and the choice of \(F_2\) itself. Any of these may be needed.

3. Decisive experiment before theory

Before trying to prove (O6) with the flow-partition method it should be checked that the method is capable of it: for cyclically 6-edge-connected cubic graphs with a 2-factor of exactly 6 odd circuits, is there always a balanced flow partition, and if so, is it always reachable by path recolourings alone (as in MS), or does one need the 0-edge/even-circuit/2-factor freedom? tools/oddness.py implements the method exactly (canonical colouring, \(H\), 2-colourings, the balance test as one min-cut) and, as a check, reproduces Jaeger’s oddness-2 theorem: on Petersen, \(J_7\) and \(J_9\) every sampled 2-factor with 2 odd circuits and every 0-edge choice gives a balanced partition (150 + 22,354 + 71,657 cases).

Test graphs: generalized Petersen graphs \(GP(n,3)\) and \(GP(n,4)\) with \(n\) odd (girth 6 or 7, so cyclic connectivity is plausibly 6 or more; they are 3-edge-colourable but have 2-factors with 4 or 6 odd circuits, which is all the fixed-\(F_2\) template sees), the flower snarks \(J_7,J_9\) (cyclically 6-edge-connected, oddness 2), and the oddness-6 snark \(R_2\) on 40 vertices of Lukoťka–Máčajová–Mazák–Škoviera (cyclic connectivity only 2, so a failure there would not contradict (O6), but a success is informative). Results are recorded in §4 as they come in.

Looking at the 11-cut witnesses concretely (straddle A): \(S\) consists of one whole odd circuit together with an arc of each of two other odd circuits, each arc containing that circuit’s \(z\), and all seven matching edges at these 15 vertices leave \(S\); the four circuit edges of the cut are the ends of the two arcs, coloured 2 on both sides by the 0-edge placement. So the obstruction is local: three “\(z\)-neighbourhoods” of the same colour glued by the matching. Moving a 0-edge along one of the two partial circuits changes both which vertex is \(z\) and the colour parity of the arc ends, so the 0-edge freedom acts directly on this cut type.

Three things stand out.

  1. The dominant obstruction at oddness 6 is new: an 11-edge cut with 7 matching edges and 4 colour-2 edges whose side contains exactly one end of each of the three paths, all of the same colour (\(k=7=c_1=c_2+q\), tight everywhere). It is impossible at oddness 4 (\(q\le2\)) and it appears even in random cyclically 6-connected graphs. It forbids exactly the two colourings in which the three ends in \(S\) agree, i.e. one of the four classes up to complement.

  2. The MS-type bad 6-cuts \((6,4,2,2,2,\{a,b\})\) do occur, in the straddle rings, for each of the three pairs, and each forbids two of the four classes. The 7-cut and \((6,4,1)\) types are rarer.

  3. Co-occurrence (tools/killsets.py, which records for each instance the set of killed classes and the cut types responsible): in the four-block straddle rings, 1 class is killed in ~30% of instances, 2 classes in ~5%, and in one instance out of 1,080 3 classes were killed (only 2 of the 8 colourings balanced). Two 11-cuts with different side patterns do coexist in one instance, so there is no “all 11-cuts have the same pattern” lemma to hope for; the combinations seen are 11+11, 11+7, 11+6(pair), 6(pair)+6(pair) and 6+7. On the 6-block rings (six12, six10, \(n=96\)–\(102\)), which contain three crossing bad-type 6-cuts, instances with only 2 balanced colourings appear at a rate of about 1 in 250, and a new type \((6,4,2,4,2,\{a,b\})\) (a 6-cut with four \(z\)’s inside) shows up.

    In every instance so far at least 1 of the 4 classes survives.

  4. Full-method run on the 6-block rings (six12, six10; 32 graphs, 250 0-edge choices each = 8,000 instances): path recolourings alone always produced a balanced partition. A second seed (SEED=23) over six12, six10, straddleA, straddleC (64 graphs, 16,000 instances) gave the same: no instance in which all 8 path colourings fail.

Summary of the evidence for the plan in §5: outcome (a) is what the data supports so far: on every cyclically 6-connected test graph, for every planted 6-odd 2-factor and 0-edge choice tried (about 35,000 instances), some recolouring of the three paths gives a balanced valuation, even though up to three of the four colouring classes can be killed. So the fixed-\(F_2\) template is not refuted at oddness 6, and the theoretical task is to prove that the five bad-cut types cannot jointly kill all four classes. Killing all four would need, e.g., a pair-type 6-cut plus two 11-cuts with different side patterns, or four 11-cuts with the four distinct patterns. Whether cyclic 6-edge-connectivity forbids such combinations is exactly what a proof of (O6) has to show; the 6-block rings (three crossing bad-type 6-cuts) are the test for it.

5. Plan

  1. Run the experiment of §3. Three possible outcomes: (a) path recolourings always suffice at \(t=3\) on cyclically 6-connected graphs: then (O6) is a case analysis of obstructions 1–3 above, and the job is to find the replacement for the parity argument; (b) they do not suffice but 0-edge/even-circuit freedom does: then the proof must first choose \(c\) well, and the experiment tells which choices work; (c) some cyclically 6-connected graph with a 6-odd 2-factor has no balanced flow partition at all: then (O6), if true, needs a different valuation family, and the template is dead at oddness 6.
  2. If (a): prove obstruction 2 impossible via uncrossing three cuts of size 6–11; prove obstruction 1 with \(P_3\) present by controlling the parity of \(|E(P_3)\cap(E_3'\cup E_4')|\), possibly by choosing \(\varepsilon_3\) or the 0-edge of \(C_5,C_6\).
  3. Either way, record the smallest configurations found; they are the “bad 6-cut” analogues that a proof must enumerate.

6. Towards a proof that the four classes cannot all be killed

Fix \(G\) cyclically 6-edge-connected cubic, \(F_2\) with 6 odd circuits, the canonical colouring \(c\), \(H\), the three paths \(P_i\) from \(z_{2i-1}\) to \(z_{2i}\), and a colouring of the even circuits of \(H\). For \(\varepsilon\in\{\pm\}^3\) let \(w_\varepsilon\) be the valuation. Write “\(S\) is tight for \(\varepsilon\)” if \(S\) is a bad cut for \(w_\varepsilon\), i.e. \(5(b_S-a_S)>3|\partial S|\) with \(b_S>a_S\).

Lemma 6.1 (tightness). If \(S\) is tight for \(\varepsilon\) then \((m,c_1,c_2,q)\) is one of \((6,4,2,2)\), \((6,4,1,3)\), \((7,5,2,3)\), \((11,7,4,3)\); in all cases \(k=b_S-a_S=c_1\), so the \(S\)-end of every colour-1 edge of \(\partial S\) is black, and except for the first type also \(k=c_2+q\), so the \(S\)-end of every colour-2 edge of \(\partial S\) is black and every \(z\in S\) on a separated path is black. In the first type the same holds because \(k=4=c_2+q\) there too. Moreover \(\partial S\subseteq E(H)\) except in type \((6,4,1,3)\), where exactly one edge of \(\partial S\) has colour 0 or 3.

Proof. The table in §2 gives the five candidates. The candidate \((6,4,2,3)\) has \(\partial S\subseteq E(H)\) with \(|\partial S|=6\) even; every even circuit of \(H\) crosses \(\partial S\) an even number of times and a path crosses it an odd number of times iff it is separated; \(q=3\) means three separated paths, an odd total, contradiction. The tightness statements are the equality cases of \(k\le c_1\) and \(k\le c_2+q\) (vertices of \(S\) not matched inside \(S\) by colour 1, resp. not covered by a colour-2 edge inside \(S\)). \(\square\)

Lemma 6.2 (alternation). Let \(S\) be tight for \(\varepsilon\) and let \(K\) be a component of \(H\) (a path or an even circuit) that crosses \(\partial S\). Then along \(K\) the crossing edges alternate in colour \(1,2,1,2,\dots\), every segment of \(K\) inside \(S\) has an odd number of vertices with both end-vertices black, and every segment outside \(S\) (between two crossings) has an odd number of vertices with both end-vertices white. For a path \(P_i\): if \(P_i\) is separated by \(S\) it crosses an odd number of times and its first and last crossing edges have colour 1; if it is not separated it crosses an even number of times (possibly zero) and, when it crosses, the crossing edge nearest one end has colour 1 and the one nearest the other end has colour 2.

Proof. The end-vertices of an inside segment are \(S\)-ends of crossing edges, hence black by Lemma 6.1; a path in \(H\) between two vertices of the same colour has even length, so the segment has an odd number of vertices and the two bounding crossing edges are an odd number of positions apart along \(K\); colours alternate along \(K\), so they differ. Outside segments are the same with white. For a path end \(z\): the end segment from \(z\) to the nearest crossing has odd length iff \(z\) has the colour appropriate to its side (black inside, white outside); the nearest crossing edge then has colour 1 (the colour of the edge at \(z\)), otherwise colour 2. A separated path has \(z\in S\) black and \(z'\notin S\) white (Lemma 6.1), so both nearest crossings have colour 1 and the number of crossings is odd. A non-separated path has its two ends of opposite colour on the same side, so exactly one end is “appropriate”, giving colours 1 and 2 at the two extremes and an even number of crossings. \(\square\)

Both lemmas were checked mechanically on every bad cut of 280 generated instances (tools/lemmas_check.py): no violation.

Corollary 6.3 (what a tight cut kills). Let \(J^*(S)\) be the set of paths that cross \(\partial S\). \(S\) is tight for exactly the colourings \(\varepsilon\) that agree with one fixed pattern \(\sigma_S\) on \(J^*(S)\): flipping any path of \(J^*(S)\) turns an inside segment black-ended into white-ended and lowers \(k\) by at least 2, below the threshold (\(k=4,5,7\) are the minimum admissible values for \(m=6,7,11\)). Flipping a path disjoint from \(\partial S\) changes nothing. Hence a tight cut kills \(2^{3-|J^*(S)|}\) colourings, i.e. (up to complement) one class if \(|J^*(S)|=3\) and two classes if \(|J^*(S)|=2\). Since \(q\ge2\), \(|J^*(S)|\ge2\). The types with \(q=3\) have \(|J^*|=3\); a pair-type cut \((6,4,2,2)\) has \(|J^*|=2\) iff the third path avoids \(\partial S\) entirely. In the generated instances most pair-type cuts are crossed by the third path (24 of 27 on the 6-rings), so they kill only one class.

The cover problem. Writing a class as \((x,y)=(\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2,\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_3)\), a codimension-2 cut on the pair \(\{1,2\}\) with sign \(s\) kills \(\{x=s\}\), on \(\{1,3\}\) kills \(\{y=s\}\), on \(\{2,3\}\) kills \(\{xy=s\}\); a codimension-3 cut kills one point. All four classes are killed only in one of these situations:

  1. two codim-2 cuts on the same pair with opposite signs;
  2. three codim-2 cuts on the three pairs with \(s_{12}s_{13}s_{23}=+1\);
  3. two codim-2 cuts on different pairs (3 classes) plus a codim-3 cut;
  4. one codim-2 cut plus two codim-3 cuts;
  5. four codim-3 cuts with the four distinct patterns.

Lemma 6.4 (situation 1 is impossible). This is the Mazzuoccolo–Steffen argument. Two codim-2 cuts \(E_3=\partial S\), \(E_4=\partial S'\) on the pair \(\{1,2\}\) with opposite signs give, after complementing, \(S\cap Z\supseteq\{z_1,z_3\}\), \(S'\cap Z\supseteq\{z_1,z_4\}\) and \(z_2\notin S\cup S'\). Their Claims 3.4 and 3.6–3.8 use only that both are 6-cuts with four colour-1 and two colour-2 edges and cyclic 6-edge-connectivity, and they give the four parts \(U_1\ni z_1\), \(U_2\ni z_2\), \(U_3\ni z_3\), \(U_4\ni z_4\) with \(|\partial(U_1,U_2)|=|\partial(U_3,U_4)|=0\) and the other four part-cuts of size 3. The final step uses that \(\partial U_1\subseteq E_3\cup E_4\subseteq E(H)\) is a 6-cut crossed an odd number of times by \(P_1\cup P_2\) and an even number of times by each even circuit of \(H\). Here the third path \(P_3\) is disjoint from \(E_3\cup E_4\) by the codimension-2 assumption, so it crosses \(\partial U_1\) zero times, and the parity contradiction stands. \(\square\)

So situation 1 cannot occur; in the data it never did. The remaining situations 2–5 all involve at least one cut of size 7 or 11 or a codim-3 pair cut, and this is where the argument has to be extended. What is known from the experiments: situations 3 and 4 have been seen partially (three classes killed, e.g. an 11-cut together with a codim-2 pair cut, or two 11-cuts and a 7-cut) but never completed. Two 11-cuts with different patterns do coexist, so situation 5 cannot be excluded by a “two 11-cuts uncross to a contradiction” lemma; it needs a statement about three or four.

Candidate lemmas (open).

  • (L-A) Two codim-3 cuts \(S,S'\) tight for classes that differ on exactly one path \(P_p\) satisfy: \(P_p\) crosses \(\partial(S\cap S')\) and \(\partial(S\cup S')\) … [to be found].   • (L-B) In situation 2, the three pair cuts \(S_{12}\ni z_1,z_3\), \(S_{13}\ni z_1,z_5\), \(S_{23}\ni z_3,z_5\) with each third path avoiding its cut: \(P_3\subseteq S_{12}\) or \(P_3\cap S_{12}=\emptyset\), and similarly for the other two. This looks like a finite case analysis of the MS kind and is the first thing to try.
  • (L-C) Crossing profiles. Let \(r_i\) be the number of times \(P_i\) crosses \(\partial S\) and \(s\) the number of colour-1 (equivalently colour-2) crossings by even circuits of \(H\). These crossing profiles are what an uncrossing argument would work with. I have not carried it out.

Where cyclic 6-edge-connectivity enters. In the above, cyclic 6-connectivity was used only to (a) exclude bad cuts of size \(\le5\) and (b) obtain the part-cut sizes in Lemma 6.4. Actually (a) needs no connectivity assumption at all: for \(m=5\) one needs \(k\ge5=c_1=m\), hence \(c_2=0\) and \(k\le q\le3\), impossible; for \(m=4\), \(k=4=c_1=m\), \(c_2=0\), \(k\le q\le 3\), impossible. Cuts of size 2 and 3 can be bad (\(k=2=c_1\), \(q\ge2\); \(k=3=c_1\), \(q=3\)), which is what happens on \(R_2\). So the catalogue of Lemma 6.1 holds for every cyclically 4-edge-connected cubic graph, and cyclic 6-edge-connectivity is used only in interaction lemmas such as Lemma 6.4.

Experiment on cyclically 5-connected (girth 5) graphs of the profiles straddleA, straddleC, six12 (CYC5=1 tools/killsets.py, 1,440 instances): the same four cut types, no others; killed-class counts \(\{0{:}757,\ 1{:}506,\ 2{:}162,\ 3{:}15\}\); no instance with all four classes killed, but 3-kills are ten times more frequent than on the cyclically 6-connected graphs. So either the four-class statement is true already at cyclic 5-connectivity, or the counterexamples are rarer than 1 in 1,440 there.

Local lemma for two codimension-3 cuts. Let \(S,S'\) be tight for \(\varepsilon,\varepsilon'\) where \(\varepsilon'\) is \(\varepsilon\) with \(P_3\) flipped (after complementing, this is the general case of two classes differing in one coordinate), and put \(A=S\cap S'\ni z_1,z_3\), \(B=V\setminus(S\cup S')\ni z_2,z_4\), \(C=S\setminus S'\ni z_5\), \(D=S'\setminus S\ni z_6\). Since \(\partial S,\partial S'\subseteq E(H)\) (for the 11- and 7-types) every edge between parts is an \(H\)-edge, bichromatic under both colourings. Then: an \(A\)–\(B\) edge lies in both cuts, so its \(A\)-end is black under \(\varepsilon\) and under \(\varepsilon'\), hence not on \(P_3\); a \(C\)–\(D\) edge lies in both cuts, its \(C\)-end is black under \(\varepsilon\) and its \(D\)-end black under \(\varepsilon'\), which is only consistent if both ends lie on \(P_3\); for \(P_3\) itself, indexing its vertices \(v_0=z_5,\dots,v_L=z_6\) (black under \(\varepsilon\) iff the index is even), an edge of \(P_3\) of type \(C\)–\(B\) or \(A\)–\(D\) has its \(C\)- or \(A\)-end at an even index, of type \(A\)–\(C\) or \(B\)–\(D\) its \(A\)- or \(D\)-end at an odd index, and \(P_3\) has no \(A\)–\(B\) edge; \(P_1,P_2\) have no \(C\)–\(D\) edge. The parities \(|\partial A|,|\partial B|\) even and \(|\partial C|,|\partial D|\) odd follow from which paths each part separates. I checked that these conditions are consistent (e.g. \(|AB|=4,|AD|=3,|CB|=2,|CD|=2,|AC|=1,|DB|=4\)), in line with the observed coexistence of two 11-cuts; a contradiction can only come from three or four cuts at once, or from an inequality on part-cuts using cyclic 6-connectivity.

7. Stress test at higher oddness: does the four-class phenomenon scale?

The proof strategy of §6 is “the tight cuts cannot jointly kill all \(2^{t-1}\) classes”. If that were a general truth for every \(t\) on cyclically 6-edge-connected graphs, the path-recolouring template would prove the 5-flow conjecture for all cyclically 6-edge-connected cubic graphs, which nobody expects to be this easy. So it is worth knowing how it fails, or fails to fail, at oddness \(8,10,12,14\): the first oddness at which the killed classes reach \(2^{t-1}\) tells where the real difficulty sits, and the cut combinations that get closest are the model for what a proof at \(t=3\) must exclude.

Correction (classes versus colourings). The “four classes up to complement” bookkeeping of §6 and the tables of §7 merge each path colouring \(\varepsilon\) with \(-\varepsilon\). That is exact only when the complement is applied to all components of \(H\). The tools keep the even circuits of \(H\) at a fixed random colouring and flip the paths only, so \(\varepsilon\) and \(-\varepsilon\) are two different valuations. Let \(s\) be the number of even-circuit segments of a tight cut \(S\) inside \(S\) (the \(s\) of the crossing profiles in §6). For the path-flipped colouring the imbalance of \(S\) becomes \(2s-k\) and that of \(V\setminus S\) becomes \(k-2s\), which is below the threshold unless \(s=0\). Hence a tight cut kills the \(2^{t-|J^*(S)|}\) colourings with its pattern, and the complementary pattern only if no even circuit crosses it. The right statistic is therefore the number of killed colourings among all \(2^t\) (with the even circuits fixed), and a failure of the path-recolouring template means all \(2^t\) are killed. run_oddness.py always tested all \(2^t\), so the headline “path recolourings never failed” is unaffected; killsets_t.py tested only the half with \(\varepsilon_1=0\), so its “worst kill of \(2^{t-1}\) classes” numbers in the table above are counts over that half; killsets.py counted a class as killed if either of its two colourings was. Uncrossing the dumped three-kill instances at oddness 6 (tools/uncross3.py) shows the difference: of the six instances, two have 6 of the 8 colourings killed (the two survivors are complementary), two have 5, one 4 and one 3. All three tools now count colourings; the tables will be redone in colouring terms.

  • The rule “the path-complement of a killed colouring is killed by \(V\setminus S\) iff no even circuit crosses \(\partial S\)” was verified on the six three-kill instances (tools/check_complement.py): no violation, and in the two 6-of-8 instances all witness cuts have \(s=0\), so there the four-class picture is exact and three of the four complementary pairs are genuinely dead.

For a surviving colouring one can force a pattern (chosen path ends inside \(S\), the others outside) and minimize \(3d(S)-5(b_S-a_S)\) by one min cut (tools/forced_cut.py). In the 6-of-8 instance threekill_straddleC_5_0_43 both survivors have forced cuts with slack 0: a 15-cut with \(k=9\) and a 10-cut with \(k=6\), i.e. \(3m=5k\) exactly. The \(m=5j\), \(k=3j\) cuts are the boundary cases of the catalogue (the inequality \(5k>3m\) excludes them, and nothing else does), and the data says the fourth pattern of situation 5 tends to be realized precisely by such a boundary cut. A proof that the fourth pattern cannot be bad therefore has no room: it must show that the best cut for that pattern has \(3m\ge5k\), with equality attainable. tools/slack_stats.py measures how often survivors sit at slack 0 (running).

So \(3m=5k\) cuts are the generic situation, not a special feature of the near-failures: the catalogue’s strict inequality \(5k>3m\) is what separates “always some colouring survives” from failure, by one edge, essentially everywhere. Any proof will have to handle the equality cases as the typical ones rather than as a degenerate corner.

8. Directed search and exact search (started 2026-09-12 evening)

Random sampling reaches 6 of 8 killed colourings at oddness 6 at a rate of about 1 in 500 instances. Two stronger tools replace it:

  • tools/climb.py: hill-climbing with annealing over the whole instance for a fixed planted 2-factor: matching 2-swaps keeping girth \(\ge6\) (cyclic 6-edge-connectivity re-checked by SAT on every accepted swap), 0-edge moves and even-component flips; objective = killed colourings, tiebreak = the survivors’ forced-cut slack; restarts after 1,500 stale steps; near-misses (\(\ge 2^t-2\) killed) are dumped as solver hints. First runs (eight climbers, 20,000 steps): 6 of 8 reached within a few hundred steps on straddleA and the random \(6C_7{+}C_8\) family, 12 of 16 at oddness 8.
  • tools/fullkill_sat.py: an exact CP-SAT model for a fixed planted 2-factor. Variables: the perfect matching (girth \(\ge6\) by binary clauses), the 0-edge of each odd circuit, the base 2-colouring of \(H\), a label in \(\{0,1,2,3\}\) per vertex propagated along \(H\)-edges, integer potentials that increase by exactly one along \(H\)-edges at nonzero-labelled vertices (this forces the nonzero label classes to be exactly the three \(z\)–\(z\) paths, since an \(H\)-cycle cannot carry a monotone potential and each label is required to contain exactly two \(z\)’s), and for each of the \(2^t\) path colourings a set \(S\) with \(5(b_S-a_S)\ge3d(S)+1\), switched on by a “kill” literal. Objective: maximize the number of kills; \(2^t\) is a template failure. Cyclic 6-edge-connectivity is added lazily: each solution is checked by SAT and any small cyclic cut \(X\) is excluded by the linear constraint \(d(X)\ge6\) on the matching variables. A proof of optimum \(<2^t\) for a given 2-factor shape would be a computer proof that the template cannot fail on that shape, for every matching at once. Warm starts from dumped near-miss instances are supported.

Results of the first exact and directed searches.

  • CP-SAT (fullkill_sat.py) was validated on the 6-of-8 instance threekill_straddleC_5_0_43: with the matching, 0-edges, base colouring and labels fixed, it proves the optimum 6 in 3 seconds once the tight-cut structure of Lemma 6.1 is added as constraints (at most \(3t-2\) crossing matching edges, \(1\) to \(2t-2\) crossing colour-2 edges, at most one other edge, every crossing colour-1 or colour-2 edge black at its \(S\)-end, \(|S|\ge6\)). Without those constraints it cannot even find the witness sets. In free search (30 minutes, 4 workers) it reaches 4 of 8 on \(6C_7\) (\(n=42\)) and on the straddleC shape, with the upper bound stuck at 8: no proof and weaker than the climber at finding kills. 9. The end graph: survivability as a not-all-equal problem

Let \(Z=\{z_1,\dots,z_{2t}\}\) and write \(c(z)\in\{B,W\}\) for the colour of an end under a path colouring. Along a path the two ends always have opposite colours (paths have an even number of vertices), so a path colouring is the same thing as an assignment \(c\colon Z\to\{B,W\}\) with \(c(z_{2i})\ne c(z_{2i-1})\), i.e. \(t\) free bits. Every tight cut \(S\) acts on the ends only through \(S\cap Z\) (Lemma 6.1: \(k=c_2+q\) is tight, so the ends inside \(S\) on separated paths are all black, and complementing \(S\) makes them all white): a tight cut with \(S\cap Z=T\) and \(|J^*(S)|=|T|\) kills exactly the colourings in which \(T\) is monochromatic. Hence, for a fixed instance, the set of killed colourings is the set of assignments violating one of the constraints

  • \(c(z_{2i-1})\neq c(z_{2i})\) for each path (always present),
  • \(\mathrm{NAE}(T)\) for each set \(T=S\cap Z\) of a tight cut \(S\) whose separated paths are exactly the paths met by \(T\) (a pair cut gives a 2-clause, i.e. \(c(z_\alpha)\ne c(z_\beta)\); an 11- or 7-cut a 3-clause),

and the template fails iff this not-all-equal instance on \(Z\) is unsatisfiable. (Cuts crossed by paths they do not separate kill fewer colourings; cuts with \(s>0\) kill only the black version, so they are “half” clauses; both only make failure harder.)

Consequences.

  1. Pair cuts alone (2-clauses). The constraint graph on \(Z\) (path edges plus pair-region edges, all “\(\neq\)”) must be non-bipartite for a failure. Its odd cycles are exactly: two pair regions on the same pair of paths with opposite signs (a triangle through a path edge: situation 1, excluded by Lemma 6.4) and, at \(t=3\), three pair regions on the three pairs with sign product \(+1\) (situation 2, a triangle or a 5-cycle through two path edges). A cycle that alternates path edges and region edges has even length, which is why the rings of blocks with two ends per block can never fail through their block cuts.
  2. Rings explained. In the 4-block ring instance with 14 of 16 killed, the blocks carry ends \(\{z_1,z_3\},\{z_3',z_5\},\dots\) and the paths join the blocks in a 4-cycle; the two survivors are exactly the two colourings that give every block one black and one white end, i.e. the two orientations of that 4-cycle of paths. In general, orienting each cycle of the “path graph on blocks” consistently gives block-balanced colourings, and block-balanced colourings survive all block cuts because their \(z\)-imbalance is \(0\) there. This is the mechanism behind the \(2^t-2\) ceilings observed at every oddness on rings.
  3. 11-cuts (3-clauses). At \(t=3\) the three free bits \(c(z_1),c(z_3),c(z_5)\) admit 8 assignments, NAE-clauses are complement-symmetric, so each 3-clause kills one of the 4 classes and a failure by 11-cuts alone needs four tight 11-cut regions \(T_1,\dots,T_4\) whose monochromatic patterns are the four classes; up to renaming, all four regions contain \(z_1\) and they realize all combinations \(\{z_3\text{ or }z_4\}\times\{z_5\text{ or }z_6\}\). The data: three of the four coexist (three instances in 1,080; two of them with all three cuts having \(s=0\)), never four. So the theorem to prove at oddness 6 is:

(T4) In a cyclically 6-edge-connected cubic graph with the canonical colouring of a 6-odd 2-factor, there are no four tight 11-cuts \(S_1,\dots,S_4\) with \(S_i\cap Z\) running through the four classes of triples (together with the mixed cases: two 11-cuts plus one pair cut, one 11-cut plus a 3-class pair configuration, and the 7-cut and \((6,4,1,3)\) variants).

The parity tool of MS applies to every set \(U\) whose boundary lies in \(E(H)\): \(|\partial U|\equiv\) number of paths separated by \(U \pmod 2\) (every path crosses \(\partial U\) an odd number of times iff separated, even circuits of \(H\) cross evenly). For two tight 11-cuts with \(s=0\) realizing adjacent classes this gives, with \(A=S\cap S'\), \(B=V\setminus(S\cup S')\), \(C=S\setminus S'\), \(D=S'\setminus S\): \(d(A),d(B)\) even, \(d(C),d(D)\) odd, \(AD+BC=AC+BD\), and \(AB+CD+(AD+BC)=11\); the colour conditions force every \(A\)–\(B\) edge onto \(P_1\cup P_2\) and every \(C\)–\(D\) edge onto \(P_3\). These are consistent for two cuts (as observed); (T4) needs the same bookkeeping on the 16 atoms of four cuts, which I have not done.

Computer-assisted attack on (T4), pure case (tools/t4_abstract.py). For four tight 11-cuts of type \((11,7,4,3)\) with \(s=0\) realizing the four classes, every edge between two of the 16 atoms is an \(H\)-edge on a path, so each path is a walk through atoms with segments (atom, length). 13. After the counterexamples: what (O6) now needs

The fixed template is dead, but the sweeps say something positive: for the 60-vertex instance exactly one of \(7^6\) 0-edge choices fails, and the same sweep is running for the 42-vertex one. The failures are isolated points. This suggests replacing plan (a) of §5 by an averaging argument over 0-edges:

(O6′) For every cyclically 6-edge-connected cubic graph with a 2-factor of six odd circuits there is a choice of 0-edges (one per odd circuit) and a 2-colouring of \(H\) that is balanced.

A proof would count, for a fixed 2-factor, the pairs (0-edge choice, colouring class) killed by tight cuts and show that fewer than \(7^6\cdot 4\) (with 7-circuits) can be killed. What is known about a single tight 11-cut \(S\) is favourable: its four colour-2 boundary edges are the ends of two arcs, so \(S\) depends on the 0-edges of the two arc circuits only through the colours of those four edges, and moving a 0-edge along an arc circuit by one position flips the colours of all its circuit edges, which destroys the tightness of every 11-cut using an arc of that circuit (the arc ends become colour 3 and leave \(H\)). Hence each tight 11-cut is tied to the 0-edge positions of its two arc circuits modulo the alternation, i.e. to a set of 0-edge choices of density at most \(1/4\) in the two coordinates concerned. Killing all four classes needs four such cuts using at least two distinct arc circuits in total; the question is whether the union of the corresponding regions of the 0-edge space can be everything, and the data (one point in \(117{,}649\)) says it is far from it. A counting proof along these lines would also have to handle the pair cuts and 7-cuts and the way \(H\) itself changes with the 0-edges (the paths are re-routed, since which circuit edges are colour 2 changes). This is the plan I would pursue next; the tools to test any intermediate claim exist (zero_sweep.py for the density of failures over 0-edges, killsets.py/killsets_t.py for the killed sets, count_general.py for configuration exclusions).

A second consequence concerns the method’s other freedom. The 0-edge-robust search (robust.py) tries to make many 0-edge choices fail at once for one 2-factor; if it stays at one failing choice, (O6′) is the right statement; if it produces a 2-factor with all \(7^6\) choices failing, the 2-factor itself must be chosen, and the oddness-6 case moves to the level of Steffen’s 2-factor arguments.

Sweep of the 42-vertex instance. Again exactly one of the \(7^6\) 0-edge choices fails, the instance’s own \((4,1,6,4,2,2)\) (zero_sweep_n42.log). Two out of two 2-factors with a failing choice have precisely one failing choice; the density of failures over the 0-edge space is \(1/117{,}649\) in both. For the averaging plan of §13 this is the best possible news; for a 0-edge-robust counterexample it is the worst, and the robust climber (robust.py, two runs of 1,500 steps from the 60-vertex instance) indeed never raised the number of failing sampled choices above one.

Two-point robustness test (tools/robust2.py). Keeping the known failing 0-edge choice \(c^*\) as a hard constraint, anneal the matching to make the neighbouring choice \(c'\) (one 0-edge moved by one step on circuit \(i\)) fail as well, with the structured-slack gradient under \(c'\). Under \(c'\) the moved circuit’s colours flip, \(H\) is re-routed and typically gains a component (16 colourings); at the start 4 of 16 are killed. Runs for all six circuits of the 42-vertex instance and two circuits of the 60-vertex one are in progress; a success would show that failures can cluster and would be the seed of a 0-edge-robust counterexample, a uniform failure would support (O6′). Results (all six neighbours of the 42-vertex instance and two of the 60-vertex one, 3,000 steps each, and 60,000 steps for the two closest neighbours): no second failing choice; the best neighbours reach 14 of 16 killed colourings while the original choice keeps failing. Almost every matching swap destroys the original failure. In graph space the failure is less isolated: of the 148 girth-preserving single matching swaps of the 42-vertex instance, 9 keep all eight colourings unbalanced and keep cyclic 6-edge-connectivity, so the counterexample sits in a small family of neighbouring counterexamples, each still failing for its single 0-edge choice. Taken together: failures are robust under some changes of the graph and fragile under every change of the 0-edges, which is what an averaging argument over 0-edges would want.

Density of the killers over the 0-edge space (tools/cut_density.py, 42-vertex instance, all \(7^6\) choices). For a fixed vertex set \(S\), “\(S\) is bad for some colouring of \(H(c)\)” is decided by summing, over the components of \(H(c)\), the absolute imbalance of the component inside \(S\). The eight witness sets are live for \(0.02\%\) to \(0.22\%\) of the choices each (24 to 260 choices out of 117,649); two, four, six and eight of them are live simultaneously for 329, 16, 1 and 1 choices, the last being the failing choice itself. The easy part of these densities is combinatorial: a 13-vertex killer is a whole circuit plus two 3-vertex arcs, and both ends of a 3-arc are colour 2 only when the arc is centred at the circuit’s \(z\), so the two arc circuits’ 0-edges are pinned (density \(1/49\approx2\%\)); the remaining factor of 50 to 100 comes from the routing of \(H\) through the other four circuits, which must place the three ends inside \(S\) with the right colours. A proof of (O6′) by averaging must capture this second factor, since the number of candidate 11-cut sets (choice of whole circuit, two arc circuits and arc extents) is in the thousands and a union bound with density \(1/49\) alone is far from sufficient.

Where the density comes from. Let \(S\) be a 13-vertex killer (whole circuit plus two 3-arcs) with its three \(z\)’s inside. Degrees in \(H\): the 13 vertices have \(2\cdot13-3=23\) \(H\)-half-edges, 11 of which cross \(\partial S\), so \(S\) contains exactly 6 internal \(H\)-edges and hence exactly \(13-6=7\) segments of \(H\). The maximum of \(|b_S-a_S|\) over all colourings is the sum over components of \(|{\rm imbalance}|\), and a segment contributes at most 1, with equality iff its length is odd. Tightness needs 7, so all seven segments have odd length and, under the killing colouring, the same sign; segments of the same path must therefore sit at mutually compatible parities of position along the path. Two of the three coordinates of the 0-edge choice are pinned by the arc centres (factor \(1/49\)); the remaining four coordinates re-route \(H\) and decide the parities of the \(7-3=4\) non-\(z\) segments relative to their \(z\)’s, each roughly a coin flip: \(1/49\cdot2^{-4}\approx1.3\cdot10^{-3}\), which is the observed order (\(2\cdot10^{-4}\) to \(2\cdot10^{-3}\)). A proof of (O6′) by averaging would have to make the coin flips rigorous (the parities are determined by how the paths thread the four free circuits, and moving a free 0-edge by one step flips the colour class of that circuit’s edges, which reroutes \(H\) there), and then control the union over the few thousand candidate sets. This is the precise shape of the remaining problem.

Checks. (i) Pinning is exact: for each 13-vertex killer of the 42-vertex instance, all live 0-edge choices (48, 36, 20 out of 117,649) have the two arc circuits’ 0-edges at the arc centres; the 15-vertex killer (a 5-arc) allows two positions on the 5-arc, as the colour-2 condition predicts. The lemma to prove (L-avg). For a cyclically 6-edge-connected cubic graph with a 2-factor of six odd circuits: for every vertex set \(S\) that can be a tight cut, the number of 0-edge choices for which \(S\) is bad for some colouring of \(H\) is at most a fraction \(\rho(S)\) of all choices, with \(\sum_S\rho(S)<1\) over all candidate \(S\) (or a refined count in which the four classes are tracked separately). The pinning gives \(\rho\le 1/49\) for two-arc 11-cuts; the data says the truth is 50 to 100 times smaller because of the parities of the segments inside \(S\) along the three paths, and this is where the proof would have to work.

The union bound, computed (tools/avg_bound.py, 42-vertex instance). Candidate tight-cut sets are unions of whole circuits and at most two arcs with the exact matching-edge count of their type; there are 42 pair-type, 44 seven-type and 10,018 eleven-type candidates, of which 1,100 eleven-cuts (and no 6- or 7-cuts) admit any 0-edge choice with the right arc-end colours and three \(z\)’s inside. Summing those admissible counts gives \(B=5{,}138{,}140\approx 10.9\cdot 4N\). So the combinatorial densities alone (pinning, \(z\)-count) are an order of magnitude short, and the routing factor is essential to an averaging proof; with the measured factor of 50 to 100 the true total would be well below \(4N\), which tools/avg_true.py estimates by sampling.

The true total closes with room. Sampling 600 of the \(7^6\) choices on the 42-vertex instance and testing all 10,104 candidate sets at each (avg_true.py): the estimated total is \(T=\sum_S \mathrm{live}(S)\,k(S)\approx 27{,}600\), i.e. \(T/4N\approx0.06\). Per sampled choice the number of live candidate sets is 0 for 87% of the choices, 1 or 2 for 12%, and at most 6. So the averaging inequality holds for this instance by a factor of about 17 once the routing factor is included, even though the instance does fail at one point. The lemma to aim for becomes quantitative and clean:

(L-avg′) For a cyclically 6-edge-connected cubic graph with a 2-factor of six odd circuits, the expected number, over a uniformly random choice of 0-edges, of tight-cut candidate sets that are bad for some colouring of \(H\), weighted by the classes they kill, is less than 4.

The data gives about \(0.24\) for this expectation on the 42-vertex instance. Since a failing choice needs at least three live cuts with complementary patterns, even the weaker statement “the expected number is less than 4” is far from tight, and any proof that captures a constant fraction of the parity constraints along the paths would suffice.

On all five instances (avg_true_all.log, 400 sampled choices each): \(T/4N = 0.013,\ 0.028,\ 0.062,\ 0.062,\ 0.223\) for the 60-, 60b-, 50-, 42- and 54-vertex instances. The averaging inequality holds with a factor of 4 to 75 on every counterexample, i.e. on the 2-factors most hostile to it that we know.

The routing factor is a product over circuits. For the 13-vertex killers of the 42-vertex instance the live 0-edge choices form near-exact product sets: killer 1 has three coordinates pinned to one position and the other three restricted to 3, 4 and 4 of the 7 positions (\(1\cdot1\cdot1\cdot3\cdot4\cdot4=48\) live choices); killer 3 likewise (\(5\cdot1\cdot4\cdot1\cdot1\cdot1=20\)); the 15-vertex killer is close to a product with sets of sizes 4, 1, 5, 2, 4, 4. The mechanism is local: when a path of \(H\) passes through a circuit \(C\) it enters at a vertex \(u\) by its matching edge, follows \(u\)’s colour-2 circuit edge to a neighbour \(w\), and leaves by \(w\)’s matching edge; which neighbour is \(w\) depends on the parity of the 0-edge position of \(C\), so moving that 0-edge by one step reroutes every path through \(C\), and it also moves \(C\)’s own path end \(z\). Hence, per free circuit, only a subset of the positions keeps a given \(S\) live, and the subsets multiply. A proof of (L-avg′) could therefore proceed circuit by circuit:

(L-circ) For a candidate tight set \(S\) and a circuit \(C\) disjoint from \(S\) that carries a path segment relevant to the tightness of \(S\), at most \(\lceil |C|/2\rceil\) positions of \(C\)’s 0-edge keep \(S\) bad for some colouring.

Two such circuits with the arc pinning \(1/49\) already give density \(\le (4/7)^2/49\approx0.7\%\) per 11-cut, against the \(B/4N\approx11\) union bound, which would bring the total below \(4N\) for the 42-vertex instance; the data suggests the truth is stronger still. This is where I would start the written proof.

Towards a proof of (L-repair). Let \(c\) fail with killers \(S_1,\dots,S_4\) (up to complement) whose \(z\)-triples \(T_i=S_i\cap Z\) are transversal to the old pairing \(P=\{\{a,a'\},\{b,b'\},\{c,c'\}\}\) (one end of each path) and realize the four classes. Let \(c'\) re-pair the ends to \(P'\ne P\), say \(P'=\{\{a,b\},\{a',b'\},\{c,c'\}\}\). A set \(S\) can kill a colouring at \(c'\) only if \(S\cap Z(c')\) is transversal to \(P'\) (two ends of one path are never both black). Triples transversal to both \(P\) and \(P'\) are exactly \(\{a,b',x\}\) and \(\{a',b,x\}\) with \(x\in\{c,c'\}\), which up to complement are two of the four classes of \(P'\). So the old killers can account for at most two of the four new classes, and the other two can be killed at \(c'\) only by sets that were not killers at \(c\): sets whose triple was non-transversal for \(P\) (they could not be tight at \(c\) because their pattern was impossible), or sets that involve the moved circuit (an arc around the new \(z\), or arc ends whose colours changed). Everything away from the moved circuit is unchanged by the move: the matching, the colours, the other \(z\)’s, hence the tightness conditions of every set not meeting the moved circuit. So (L-repair) reduces to a local statement: a single 0-edge move on circuit \(C\) cannot create, out of sets that were not tight at \(c\), tight cuts for both of the two new classes that the old killers cannot reach, unless it undoes the tightness of the old ones. The data says this never happens (291 of 291), and the structure of tight sets (§13: whole circuits plus two arcs, arcs centred on \(z\)’s) makes it a finite case analysis around \(C\): the only sets whose status changes are those with an arc on \(C\) or with \(z_C\) inside a whole circuit… this is the next thing to write down carefully.

Lemma 13.1 (old killers after a re-pairing). Let \(c\) and \(c'\) be 0-edge choices such that \(H(c)\) and \(H(c')\) both consist of three paths, with pairings \(P\ne P'\) of the six odd circuits. Let \(S\) be a set that is a tight 11- or 7-cut at \(c\) (so \(S\cap Z(c)\) is a triple transversal to \(P\)) and suppose \(S\cap Z(c')\) consists of the \(z\)’s of the same three circuits. Then \(S\) can kill at \(c'\) only a class among two fixed classes of \(P'\), the same two for every such \(S\). Proof. Write \(P=\{\{a,a'\},\{b,b'\},\{c_1,c_1'\}\}\). Two distinct perfect matchings of six elements share at most one pair; after renaming, \(P'=\{\{a,b\},\{a',b'\},\{c_1,c_1'\}\}\) (if they share no pair the same count applies to the crossed pairs). A triple that kills at \(c'\) must be transversal to \(P'\), since two ends of one path have opposite colours. Triples transversal to both \(P\) and \(P'\) are \(\{a,b',x\}\) and \(\{a',b,x\}\) with \(x\in\{c_1,c_1'\}\), which up to complement are two classes of \(P'\). \(\square\)

So after a re-pairing at least two of the four new classes can be killed only by sets that were not killers at \(c\) (newborn killers, necessarily involving the moved circuit, since nothing else changed) or by pair-type cuts, whose kill pattern is not a transversal triple. (L-repair) is thereby reduced to: newborn 11/7-cut killers cannot cover two classes by themselves after a single move, and pair-type killers behave (the 54-vertex instance shows old pair cuts keeping three classes killed after a re-pairing, never four).

Lemma 13.2 (a 0-edge move is a cyclic shift of the visits). Let \(C=v_0v_1\cdots v_{L-1}\) be an odd circuit with 0-edge \(e_j=v_jv_{j+1}\), so \(z_C=v_j\), and the colour-2 edges of \(C\) are \(e_{j+1},e_{j+3},\dots,e_{j+L-2}\); the paths of \(H\) visit \(C\) along these edges, the \(k\)-th visit being \((v_{j+2k-1},v_{j+2k})\), \(k=1,\dots,(L-1)/2\), entered and left by the matching edges at its two vertices. Moving the 0-edge to \(e_{j+1}\) makes \(z_C=v_{j+1}\) and the colour-2 edges \(e_{j+2},e_{j+4},\dots,e_{j+L-1}\): the visits become \((v_{j+2k},v_{j+2k+1})\), i.e. each new visit joins the exit vertex of the old \(k\)-th visit to the entry vertex of the old \((k{+}1)\)-st, and the last joins the old last exit to the old \(z_C\); the old first entry \(v_{j+1}\) becomes the new \(z_C\). Everything outside \(C\) is unchanged. (Verified on all 126 (circuit, position) cases of the 42-vertex instance.) Proof. Immediate from the definition of the canonical colouring. \(\square\)

Consequently the effect of the move on \(H\) is determined by the external connection pattern at \(C\): the \(L\) matching edges at \(C\) lead into pieces of \(H\) that either return to \(C\) at another attachment vertex or end at one of the other five \(z\)’s; the move re-glues these pieces by the shift above. Whether the pairing of the six ends changes is a finite question about this pattern, which is the right formulation of the existence statement for (L-repair).

The two-position shift is a rotation. Let \(C=(a_0a_1\cdots a_{L-1})\) be an odd circuit with \(z_C=a_0\) (indices relative to the 0-edge \(a_0a_1\)), so the visits are \((a_1a_2),(a_3a_4),\dots,(a_{L-2}a_{L-1})\). Moving the 0-edge to \(a_2a_3\) makes \(z_C=a_2\) and the visits \((a_0a_1),(a_3a_4),\dots\): exactly one visit changes, \(a_1a_2\) is replaced by \(a_0a_1\), and everything else in \(H\) (all other visits of \(C\), all other circuits, the matching) is unchanged. In terms of the paths this is a Pósa rotation at the end \(a_0\): if \(P\) is the path ending at \(a_0\) and \(P''=E_2\cdots a_2a_1\cdots E_1\) is the path through the visit \(a_1a_2\) (write \(\pi_1=a_1\cdots E_1\) and \(\pi_2=E_2\cdots a_2\) for its two halves), the new paths are \(Q=P+a_0a_1+\pi_1\) (ends \(E\), \(E_1\)) and \(Q''=\pi_2\) (ends \(E_2\), \(a_2\)), the third path \(R\) is untouched. The move re-pairs the ends iff \(P''\ne P\); if \(P''=P\) it either preserves the pairing or splits off an even circuit. The \(-2\) shift is the mirror image with the visit \(a_{L-2}a_{L-1}\). (One-position shifts change all three visits at once, Lemma 13.2, which is why their coupling looked opaque.)

Parity facts. Every path of \(H\) has an even number of vertices (its edges alternate matching, colour 2, …, matching), so its two ends have opposite colours in every admissible colouring; the same holds for \(P\), \(\pi_1\), \(\pi_2\): a piece from a \(z\) to an attachment vertex, or between two attachment vertices, has odd length. Hence in the four colourings admissible at both \(c\) and \(c'\), and in all the following, \(\varepsilon(a_1)=\neg\varepsilon(E_1)\), \(\varepsilon(a_0)=\neg\varepsilon(E)\), \(\varepsilon(a_2)=\neg\varepsilon(E_2)\).

Lemma 16.1 (shared colourings). A vertex 2-colouring is admissible at \(c\) (proper on \(H(c)\)) and at \(c'\) iff it is proper on the common part of \(H(c)\) and \(H(c')\) and \(\varepsilon(a_0)=\varepsilon(a_2)\). The four colourings admissible at both are the same vertex colourings, and balancedness is a property of the vertex colouring alone (\(5|b_S-a_S|\le3|\partial S|\) for all \(S\)), so their status is identical at \(c\) and \(c'\). The colourings admissible only at \(c\) are those with \(\varepsilon(a_0)=\varepsilon(a_1)\) (the removed edge \(a_0a_1\) … the added edge \(a_0a_1\) is monochromatic), those admissible only at \(c'\) have \(\varepsilon(a_1)=\varepsilon(a_2)\) (the removed edge \(a_1a_2\) monochromatic). Flipping the colours on \(\pi_1\) is a bijection between the \(c\)-only and the \(c'\)-only colourings. Proof. \(H(c)\cap H(c')\) misses only the edges \(a_1a_2\) and \(a_0a_1\); a colouring proper on the common part is proper on \(a_1a_2\) iff \(\varepsilon(a_1)\ne\varepsilon(a_2)\) and on \(a_0a_1\) iff \(\varepsilon(a_0)\ne\varepsilon(a_1)\); both hold iff \(\varepsilon(a_0)=\varepsilon(a_2)\). Flipping \(\pi_1\) changes \(\varepsilon(a_1)\) and nothing at \(a_0,a_2\), and keeps the colouring proper on the common part (the only common edges leaving \(\pi_1\) are at \(a_1\), namely \(a_1a_2\) and \(a_0a_1\), which are not common). \(\square\) (Verified on all two-position re-pairing shifts of the archives by tools/shift2.py: no admissibility mismatch.)

Consequently, writing \(\varepsilon_0\) for a \(c\)-only colouring, the eight \(c\)-colourings are \(\varepsilon_0\) twisted by the group generated by the flips of \(P\), \(\pi_1\cup\pi_2\) and \(R\), the eight \(c'\)-colourings are \(\varepsilon_0^{\pi_1}\) twisted by the flips of \(P\cup\pi_1\), \(\pi_2\), \(R\), and the two families share four colourings. Up to complement, \(c\) and \(c'\) together have six classes, arranged in a \(3\times2\) grid: with \(E_2\) black as the representative, the black \(z\) on the \(P/\pi_1\) side is \(a_0\) (row admissible at \(c\) only), \(E\) (row shared), or \(E_1\) (row admissible at \(c'\) only), and the black end of \(R\) is \(F\) or \(F'\). The rows \(a_0\) and \(E\) are the four classes of \(c\), the rows \(E\) and \(E_1\) the four classes of \(c'\); the row-\(E\) classes are exactly the reach classes of Lemma 13.1 and the row-\(E_1\) classes the split classes. Both \(c\) and \(c'\) fail iff all six grid classes are killed. So (L-repair) for two-position shifts is the statement that the six colourings \(\varepsilon_{x,y}\), \(x\in\{a_0,E,E_1\}\), \(y\in\{F,F'\}\) cannot all be bad; it involves no path structure beyond the four pieces \(P,\pi_1,\pi_2,R\) and is symmetric under \(c\leftrightarrow c'\).

Lemma 16.2 (killers of the \(c\)-only classes die). Let \(S\) be a tight 11- or 7-cut for a \(c\)-only colouring \(\varepsilon\) (row \(a_0\)), taken on the side containing the black \(z\)’s. Then \(a_0,a_1,a_{L-1}\in S\) and \(S\) is not bad for \(\varepsilon^{\pi_1}\); symmetrically a tight 11- or 7-cut for a \(c'\)-only colouring is not bad for the corresponding \(c\)-only colouring. Proof. Tightness forces every boundary edge of \(S\) to be an \(H(c)\)-edge, so \(S\cap C\) is a union of arcs whose two boundary circuit edges are visits. An arc containing \(a_0\) has its right boundary edge among \((a_1a_2),(a_3a_4),\dots\) and its left one among the same edges, hence contains \(a_1\) and \(a_{L-1}\). Tightness also says \(b_S-a_S\) equals the number of segments of \(H(c)\) inside \(S\) (7 for an 11-cut, 5 for a 7-cut) and every segment has two black ends. Write \(p=\delta_\varepsilon(S\cap\pi_1)\) for the black-minus-white count of \(S\) on \(\pi_1\): it is the number of segments meeting \(\pi_1\) — each segment inside \(\pi_1\) contributes \(+1\), and the segment through \(a_1\) (which exists, \(a_1\in S\) being black since \(\varepsilon(a_1)=\varepsilon(a_0)\)) meets \(\pi_1\) in a subpath from the black \(a_1\) to a black segment end, also \(+1\). So \(1\le p\le 6\) (the segment through \(a_0\) lies on \(P\)), and \(\delta_{\varepsilon^{\pi_1}}(S)=\delta_\varepsilon(S)-2p\) lies in \([-5,5]\) for an 11-cut (\([-3,3]\) for a 7-cut), which is not bad. \(\square\)

For pair cuts the lemma needs a hypothesis: a \((6,4,2,2)\) pair cut containing \(a_0\), or meeting \(\pi_1\), dies for the same reason; one avoiding \(a_0\) and \(\pi_1\) altogether is untouched by the flip and stays bad; and a \((6,4,1,3)\) cut whose non-\(H\) boundary edge is \(a_0a_1\) itself (so \(a_0\in S\), \(a_1\notin S\)) becomes a \((6,4,2,2)\) pair cut at \(c'\). This is the pair-cut case of §13 in its precise form.

So for two-position shifts the picture is completely sharp: the row-\(E\) classes are killed by old sets which remain tight (their colourings are unchanged), the row-\(a_0\) classes by sets that are not bad for any \(c'\)-colouring of the grid, and the row-\(E_1\) classes can be killed only by sets that are not bad for any \(c\)-colouring of the grid (newborn). (L-repair) for these shifts says: the rows \(a_0\) and \(E_1\) cannot both be fully killed (given the shared row is). Since the statement is symmetric it cannot follow from the local structure at \(C\) alone; it must be an incompatibility between a tight set for \(\varepsilon_{a_0,y}\) and one for \(\varepsilon_{E_1,y'}\) in the presence of the tight sets for the shared row. What the pieces look like inside these sets, in positions counted from the black \(z\): on \(\pi_2\) (from \(E_2\)) and on \(R\) (from \(F\) or \(F'\)) every killer meets the piece in intervals \([\text{even},\text{even}]\); on \(P\) (from \(a_0\)) the rows \(a_0\) and \(E_1\) use \([\text{even},\text{even}]\) intervals (row \(a_0\) with one starting at \(a_0\); row \(E_1\) may or may not contain \(a_0\)), the row \(E\) uses \([\text{odd},\text{odd}]\) intervals ending at \(E\); on \(\pi_1\) (from \(a_1\)) the rows \(a_0\) and \(E\) use \([\text{even},\text{even}]\) intervals, row \(a_0\) with one starting at \(a_1\), and the row \(E_1\) uses \([\text{odd},\text{odd}]\) intervals with one ending at \(E_1\). On \(C\) itself: a row-\(a_0\) killer contains \(a_{L-1},a_0,a_1\) (whole \(C\) or a \(z\)-arc), a row-\(E\) killer avoids \(a_0\) and meets \(C\) in \(\emptyset\) or even arcs with visit boundaries at \(c\) (\(\{a_2,a_3\},\{a_4,a_5\},\{a_2,\dots,a_5\}\) for \(L=7\)), a row-\(E_1\) killer avoids \(a_1,a_2,a_3\) and meets \(C\) in \(\emptyset\), \(\{a_4,a_5\}\), \(\{a_6,a_0\}\) or \(\{a_4,a_5,a_6,a_0\}\).

Existence of a re-pairing two-position shift. The \(+2\) shift on \(C\) re-pairs iff the first visit \((a_1a_2)\) lies on a path other than the one ending at \(z_C\), the \(-2\) shift iff the last visit does. A failing choice with no re-pairing two-position shift would have, on every circuit, both extreme visits on the circuit’s own \(z\)-path; tools/shift2.py counts the re-pairing two-position shifts of every archived failing choice (see below).

Grid statistics on random choices (tools/gridsample.py: random 0-edge choices, every re-pairing two-position shift with three paths at both ends, badness of the six grid colourings by min-cut; four instances, 33,000 shifts). What this says about (L-repair). A random admissible colouring is bad with probability of a few per cent, the four classes of a choice are killed nearly independently, so a failing choice is a coincidence of four rare events (about \(10^{-5}\) per choice, matching the 0–7 failing choices per swept 2-factor), and a violation of (L-repair) at a two-position shift is a coincidence of six: the two shared classes plus the two \(c\)-only and the two \(c'\)-only ones. Nothing in the data so far separates “law” from “coincidence of six rare events” — under independence one would expect one violation per \(10^{8}\)–\(10^{9}\) random shifts, far beyond sampling, and the 0 of 583 re-pairing moves from failing choices is consistent with both (the prediction under independence is about one violation per few hundred such moves only if the newborn classes were as likely as generic ones; they are killed in about 1 % of these moves each, so the expected number of violations in 583 moves is below 0.1). The constructive test is a climber that optimizes the six grid colourings together (tools/climb12.py: matching 2-swaps, objective = number of bad grid classes then the structured slack of the survivors; a full kill with different pairings and three paths at both choices is a violation); runs from the failing choices (five of six classes bad, slack 4 remaining on the 50-vertex configuration) and from the \([2,6,2,4,1,4]\) configuration above are in progress (tools/climb12_*.log).

Atom-count relaxation for the grid (tools/grid_count.py): the §10 model generalized from paths to the four pieces \(P,\pi_1,\pi_2,R\) (walks through the \(2^J\) atoms, crossing colours forced by the piece’s start colour under each cut’s colouring, the glue edges \(a_1a_2\in H(c)\) and \(a_0a_1\in H(c')\) added explicitly, free membership of \(a_0\) in the row-\(E_1\) cuts and of \(a_2\) in the row-\(a_0\) cuts, matching and visit parities per atom for both \(H(c)\) and \(H(c')\), circuit classes with \(C\)’s class containing the glue crossings, \(N\ge42\), and the union facts on single atoms, pairs, the classical uncrossing sets and lazily on violated unions). Rows \(a_0,E_1\) alone: FEASIBLE (as it must be, given the configuration above). All three rows: running (tools/grid_count_all.log); INFEASIBLE would prove (L-repair) for two-position shifts with 11-cut killers, FEASIBLE would give an abstract configuration to realize.

Class kills at a fixed choice are nearly independent (tools/classpairs.py on the exhaustive live-set dump of the 42-vertex counterexample, 117,649 choices): each class is killed at 2.0–2.2 % of the choices; pairs of classes are killed together 0.9–1.8 times as often as independence predicts (adjacent classes, differing in one path flip, 0.87, 1.24, 1.58, 1.78; opposite classes 1.05, 1.08); one choice has all four. So there is no class-level anticorrelation to build a coupling on; the apparent anticorrelation between the rows \(a_0\) and \(E\) in the grid samples is clustering (every shift of a base reuses its colourings).

Three-row relaxation: inconclusive. grid_count.py a0,E,E1 returned FEASIBLE after two rounds with a degenerate solution (one atom of 500 vertices, eight atoms of 2–3 vertices, \(N=522\)): the count relaxation has no constraint that prevents piling the graph into one atom, and the lazily separated union facts do not bite there. As built it cannot prove (L-repair); making it decisive would need constraints tying the pieces’ lengths to the atoms (e.g. the visit structure of every circuit inside a set), which is the same missing “geometry of the circuits” noted in §10.

Where this leaves the local approach. Under the independence picture, (L-repair) for every re-pairing move is a statement whose violations are coincidences of six rare events, and the weaker statement actually needed for (O6′) — from a failing choice, some re-pairing move repairs — needs a coincidence of about sixteen (the two newborn classes at each of the five to eight re-pairing two-position shifts). Both are true with overwhelming probability and neither has a visible deterministic mechanism: the newborn killers of different shifts are unrelated tight sets of \(G\), and \(G\) has hundreds of live candidate sets (464 on the 42-vertex counterexample), so no counting of candidates forbids their alignment. The one lever left is the climber: a constructed violation of (L-repair) would settle the “law or coincidence” question negatively and redirect the effort (multi-step moves, or the union of colourings over a whole axis); failure of long climbs would leave it open. A general proof of (O6) along these lines needs an idea that is not in the data.

18.0 What the review says

Three things stand out when §§1–17, the mod-5 notes and the survey are read together.

  1. Both routes study the same object. Jaeger’s balanced valuation \(w=\pm\tfrac53\) (§1), the half-set \(S\) of Lemma C in mod5-orientation.md (\(\bigl||X\cap S|-|X|/2\bigr|\le0.3\,d_G(X)\)) and the \(V_1/V_2\) classes of a Matamala–Zamora even \((1,2)\)-factor are one and the same bipartition of \(V\): the 5-flow conjecture for cubic graphs is the statement that some \(\pm1\) vector \(\chi\) on \(V\) has \(|\chi(X)|\le\tfrac35 d(X)\) for every \(X\); Seymour’s theorem is the same statement with \(\tfrac23\), 3-edge-colourability is \(\tfrac12\). The oddness route restricts \(\chi\) to the MS family (proper colourings of \(H\)), the mod-5 route tried to build \(\chi\) by LTWZ induction.
  2. The oddness route died of a rare event, not of a structural obstruction. The template fails at isolated 0-edge choices because four tight 11-cuts coincide; (L-repair) is “six rare events do not coincide”. Statements of that kind are true with overwhelming probability and have no mechanism. The lesson of §17 (test against the null model) applies to every continuation along those lines, so the ideas below avoid statements of the form “a coincidence does not happen”.
  3. Every failure ever found is an 11-cut failure. Pair 6-cuts and 7-cuts never completed a cover, and §10 proves most of that. This is the opening for 18.1.

18.1 Idea A — a theorem within reach: circular flow number \(\le 11/2\) at oddness 6

Balanced valuations work for real \(r\): a cubic graph has a circular nowhere-zero \(r\)-flow if some bipartition has \(r\,|b_X-a_X|\le(r-2)\,d(X)\) for all \(X\). Redo the catalogue of §2 for general \(r\). The constraints \(k\le c_1\), \(k\le c_2+q\), \(c_1+c_2\le m\), \(q\le3\), \(k\equiv m\) give \(k\le(m+3)/2\) with the parity of \(m\), so the extremal ratios \(k/m\) are

\(m\) 6 7 8 9 10 11 12 13 14
max \(k\) 4 5 4 5 6 7 6 7 8
\(k/m\) .667 .714 .5 .556 .6 .636 .5 .538 .571

A set is bad for \(r\) iff \(k/m>(r-2)/r\). At \(r=5\) the threshold is \(.6\) and the bad types are \((6,4)\), \((7,5)\), \((11,7)\). At \(r=\tfrac{11}2\) the threshold is exactly \(7/11\): the 11-cuts are no longer bad, and the only bad cuts are the 6-cuts with \(k=4\) and the 7-cuts with \(k=5\). For \(5<r<\tfrac{11}2\) nothing changes relative to \(r=5\) (the template jumps from 5 to \(5.5\), there is nothing in between).

Check on the archives (tools/template_ratio.py: exact \(\max_S|b_S-a_S|/d(S)\) per colouring by Dinkelbach iteration on min-cuts): on all 26 archived template failures (six in data/, twenty tools/fullkill_*.json, 42–78 vertices), at the failing 0-edge choice the best colouring has worst ratio exactly \(7/11\), i.e. the template yields a circular \(\tfrac{11}2\)-flow there; the only other value that occurs is \(2/3\) (pair cuts, on the 54- and 78-vertex families, never for all colourings).

So the statement

(C6) Every cyclically 6-edge-connected cubic graph with a 2-factor of at most six odd circuits has circular flow number at most \(\tfrac{11}2\), for every such 2-factor and every choice of 0-edges.

is exactly “the four classes cannot be covered by 6-cuts and 7-cuts”, and §10 already has, in the pure case, situation 1 (12/12 infeasible), situation 2 (32/32), situation 3 with a 7-cut point (Lemma 10.2), situation 4 with two 7-cut points (Lemma 10.2) and four 7-cuts (Lemma 10.4). To my knowledge nothing below 6 is known for oddness 6 (literature check 2026-09-19: Steffen’s cyclic-connectivity theorem needs connectivity 12 at oddness 6; no general bound \(6-\varepsilon\) is known), so (C6) would be a new theorem, and it has the right shape: no rare events, only the deterministic exclusions that the relaxation of §10 is able to prove. Remaining work: 1. point-killers of type \((6,4,1,3)\) (the 6-cut with one non-\(H\) boundary edge) are not in the enumeration of §10: add them as a third kind of point to enum_covers.py; 2. the impure cases: even circuits of \(H\) crossing the cuts (\(s>0\)) and pair cuts of codimension 3 (\(r_3\ge2\) in (L-C) of §6); 3. \(H\) with even circuits (eight or more colourings per class; more freedom, should only help, but the cover problem has to be restated); 4. the six “7-heavy” situation-5 mixtures of §10 were undecided only because they contained 11-cuts or timed out; re-run the pure-7 and 7/(6,4,1,3) mixtures with a long time limit; 5. hand proofs in the style of Lemma 10.3 for a written version.

18.2 Idea B — measure the distance from the template to the truth (“defect” experiment)

For a fixed 2-factor \(F_2\) with matching \(M\), every nowhere-zero \(\mathbb Z_5\)-flow of \(G\) is visible: contracting the circuits, a flow is a map \(\varphi\colon M\to\mathbb Z_5^*\) with zero net sum at every circuit such that the partial sums of \(\varphi\) around each circuit omit a residue (the omitted residue fixes the additive constant of the flow on the circuit). So fixing the 2-factor loses nothing; what loses is the MS sub-family, in which the partial sums alternate between two values except at one 0-edge per odd circuit. Between “one defect per odd circuit” and “all flows” there is a hierarchy that nobody has looked at.

Experiment (cheap, ILP or SAT): on the 26 failing instances at their failing choices, and on random choices, compute the minimum number of monochromatic \(H\)-edges over all balanced \(\pm\tfrac53\) bipartitions (equivalently the Hamming distance from the template family to a true solution), and record where the defects sit relative to the four killing 11-cuts. If the answer is always 1 or 2 and the defects sit on the killers’ boundaries, the natural conjecture is “template + one extra defect always works”, whose obstruction catalogue (the analogue of §2 with \(q\le4\)) can be computed exactly as before. This replaces the search over 0-edge moves (which change the colourings globally and produced the rare-event picture) by a local enlargement of the family.

18.3 Idea C — the zonotope form and rounding

By 18.0(1), with \(Z(G)=\sum_{uv\in E}[-(e_u-e_v),\,e_u-e_v]=\{w:\ |w(X)|\le d(X)\ \forall X\}\), the conjecture is: \(\tfrac35Z(G)\) contains a \(\pm1\) vector. This is a discrepancy problem over a submodular constraint family, and the standard machinery of that field has not been tried on flows:

  • Iterative rounding / Lovett–Meka walk. Start at \(w=0\), move inside \(Z(G)\cap[-\tfrac53,\tfrac53]^V\), freezing coordinates that reach \(\pm\tfrac53\) and staying on tight cuts. Tight sets are closed under union and intersection (\(d\) submodular, \(w\) modular), so the active cut constraints reduce to a chain, and the walk is stuck only when every link of the chain contains exactly one unfrozen vertex; at such a point every coordinate lies in \(\tfrac13\mathbb Z\). A first target is a rounding proof of a known bound (8-flow: \(r=8\), \(w=\pm\tfrac43\); then 6), because a rounding proof does not factor the group and therefore does not stop at 6 for arithmetic reasons (survey §7, item 1).

26. The quadrant lemma: all 300 pair exclusions by hand (2026-09-20)

Lemma 26.1 (quadrant lemma). Let \(G\) be cyclically 6-edge-connected cubic on \(n\ge10\) vertices with the canonical colouring of a 2-factor with six odd circuits. There are no two sets \(S,T\subseteq V\) with \(\partial S,\partial T\subseteq E(H)\), each boundary consisting of four colour-1 and two colour-2 edges, such that the four quadrants \(S\cap T\), \(S\setminus T\), \(T\setminus S\), \(V\setminus(S\cup T)\) contain \(1,1,1,3\) of the six vertices \(z_1,\dots,z_6\) in some order.

Proof. Replacing \(S\) or \(T\) by its complement changes neither the boundaries nor their colours, and permutes the quadrants transitively; so let \(A=S\cap T\), \(B=S\setminus T\), \(C=T\setminus S\) contain one \(z\) each and \(D=V\setminus(S\cup T)\) three. All edges between quadrants are \(H\)-edges, so the two circuit neighbours of a \(z\) (joined to it by edges of colours 0 and 3) lie in its quadrant: \(|A|,|B|,|C|\ge3\), \(|D|\ge9\).

Parity. For a union \(X\) of quadrants let \(E_i(X)\) be the number of colour-\(i\) edges in \(\partial X\). The matching pairs up the vertices of \(X\) not matched outside, so \(|X|\equiv E_1(X)\); the colour-2 edges inside \(X\) pair up all vertices of \(X\) except the \(z\)’s and the ends of boundary colour-2 edges, so \(|X|\equiv E_2(X)+z(X)\). Hence \(d(X)=E_1+E_2\equiv z(X)\pmod2\): \(d(A),d(B),d(C),d(D)\) are odd.

Sizes. The girth is at least 6, so a set of at most 5 vertices is acyclic and has \(d=|X|+2\); a set \(X\) with \(|X|,|V\setminus X|\ge4\) has \(d(X)\ge6\). Hence \(d(A),d(B),d(C)\in\{5\}\cup\{7,9,\dots\}\), with \(d=5\) exactly when the quadrant is \(\{u,z,w\}\), a \(z\) with its two circuit neighbours (“3-atom”), and \(d(D)\ge7\). Submodularity, \(d(A)+d(D)+2e(B,C)=d(S)+d(T)=12\), gives \(d(A)=5\), \(d(D)=7\), \(e(B,C)=0\). With \(a=e(A,B)\), \(b=e(A,C)\), \(c=e(A,D)\): \(a+b+c=5\); \(6=d(S)=b+c+e(B,D)\) and \(6=d(T)=a+c+e(C,D)\), so \(d(B)=a+e(B,D)=2a+1\) and \(d(C)=2b+1\); both are at least 5, so \(a,b\ge2\) and \((a,b,c)\in\{(2,2,1),(2,3,0),(3,2,0)\}\).

3-atoms. In a 3-atom \(\{u,z,w\}\) the five boundary edges are the three matching edges and the two other circuit edges at \(u\) and \(w\), which have colour 2 (next to \(z\) the circuit reads \(\dots,2,3,[z],0,2,\dots\)) and end on the same odd circuit; so no colour-2 edge joins two 3-atoms (they belong to different circuits).

Case \((2,2,1)\). \(d(B)=d(C)=5\): \(A,B,C\) are 3-atoms, the two colour-2 edges of \(A\) must go to \(D\), but \(c=1\). Case \((2,3,0)\). \(B\) is a 3-atom and \(c=0\), so the two colour-2 edges of \(A\) go to \(C\), and the \(A\)–\(B\) edges are two matching edges. Then \(B\) sends one matching edge and two colour-2 edges to \(D\), and \(\partial S=E(A,C)\cup E(B,D)\) contains only \(1+1=2\) colour-1 edges instead of 4. Case \((3,2,0)\) is the same with \(T\) and \(C\). \(\square\)

The proof uses neither the paths nor the colourings: only the two boundaries, their colour counts, the position of the \(z\)’s and the structure of 3-atoms.

Skeleton of a hand proof for the triples (no \((6,4,1,3)\)-cut involved). Three cuts of sizes \(m_1,m_2,m_3\) with all boundaries in \(E(H)\), the six \(z\)’s in six different atoms of the cube \(\{0,1\}^3\). Every \(z\)-atom has odd boundary \(\ge5\), equal to 5 iff it is a 3-atom; the \(t\) 3-atoms emit \(2t\) distinct colour-2 edges (none joins two 3-atoms), so \(2t\le c_2(1)+c_2(2)+c_2(3)\le6\) and \(t\le3\). Summing over atoms, \(42-2t\le\sum_{z\text{-atoms}}d\le2\,|\partial S_1\cup\partial S_2\cup\partial S_3|\le2(m_1+m_2+m_3)\).
